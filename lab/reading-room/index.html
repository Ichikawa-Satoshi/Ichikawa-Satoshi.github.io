Structural Transformation and Service Trade in Japan

Working paper
Abstract

This paper studies how service imports shape structural transformation in Japan. Using prefectural data for 2008–2018 and instrumenting import exposure with foreign export growth, I find that greater exposure to service imports is associated with a slower shift of employment toward services. A move from the 25th to the 75th percentile of exposure corresponds to a 0.14 percentage-point smaller annual increase in the service employment share, about 37 percent of the average annual increase in the sample. When services are divided by task content into remote and local services, the negative association is concentrated in local services. To interpret this pattern, I develop a three-sector small open economy model that reflects Japan’s input–output structure. In the calibrated model, cheaper remote imports shift household spending from local to remote services. This shift lowers the local employment share. In remote services, employment gains from household demand are largely offset by losses in jobs serving exports, leaving the overall service employment share below its no-shock level.

Firm Organization, Reporting Networks, and Productivity

with Tatsuro Senga

Working paper
Abstract

How do reporting relationships within a firm relate to its productivity? We use large language models to reconstruct reporting networks from published organization charts for 3,450 publicly listed firms in Japan. The charts cover four survey years between 2002 and 2010. Larger firms have more departments, more managerial layers, and more units reporting to each manager. They also have longer reporting paths. Even among firms of similar size and structure, however, some have much shorter paths from front-line units to the rest of the firm. We measure this proximity using reporting closeness. After accounting for firm characteristics and hierarchy, a one-standard-deviation difference in reporting closeness is associated with 4.9% higher revenue-based productivity. To explain why these paths might matter, we extend Garicano’s (2000) model to allow departments in the same layer to specialize in different problems. When a department needs expertise from another division, its request travels along the reporting path. A shorter path involves fewer managers, so the firm can handle more requests with the same amount of managerial time.
